CASE FILE 001 • INVESTIGATION

WHY DO CONSPIRACY THEORIES REFUSE TO DIE?

Some conspiracy theories disappear almost immediately. Others survive for years, decades, or generations. This investigation examines why — using psychological research, misinformation research, documented history, and primary government sources.

Investigative Finding

Conspiracy theories survive for more than one reason.

Research points to a combination of uncertainty, attempts to understand threatening events, social identity, distrust, repetition, and the difficulty of correcting misinformation once it becomes familiar. At the same time, history contains genuine examples of secret or unethical institutional conduct. The existence of real conspiracies, however, does not establish the truth of unrelated claims. Each claim still requires its own evidence.

01 / THE QUESTION

Why are some conspiracy theories so difficult to eliminate?

Conspiracy theories are often treated as though they are simply the result of people refusing to accept facts. Psychological research suggests the picture is more complicated.

Researchers Karen Douglas, Robbie Sutton, and Aleksandra Cichocka describe conspiracy beliefs as potentially connected to three broad categories of psychological motives: epistemic motives involving understanding, existential motives involving security and control, and social motives involving the individual or group. [1]

Evidence Check A conspiracy theory can therefore provide more than an explanation. It can potentially provide certainty, meaning, group identity, or a sense that confusing events have an understandable cause.
02 / UNCERTAINTY & CONTROL

Uncertain events create a demand for explanations.

Major events are frequently complicated. Wars, assassinations, disease outbreaks, economic crashes, political scandals, technological changes, and intelligence operations may involve incomplete information and competing explanations.

Research on conspiracy belief has examined how people's desire for understanding, certainty, security, and control can contribute to the appeal of conspiratorial explanations. [1]

That does not mean uncertainty automatically creates conspiracy beliefs. It means uncertainty can create conditions in which a simple, intentional explanation may become psychologically attractive.

03 / REPETITION

Familiarity can feel like evidence.

One of the strongest findings relevant to misinformation is the illusory truth effect: repeated information can feel more truthful simply because it has become familiar.

A 2023 preregistered study found that repetition increased participants' truth judgments of conspiracy statements. [2]

A broader 2024 research review concluded that repetition can increase belief in misinformation, including fake news and conspiracy beliefs, and that the effect can occur even for implausible statements or statements that conflict with prior knowledge. [3]

A registered replication published in 2026 found that when conspiracy statements and ordinary trivia statements were matched for plausibility, repetition affected their perceived truth similarly. [4]

Investigative Implication Seeing the same claim repeatedly is not independent confirmation. Ten websites repeating one unsupported allegation may ultimately trace back to the same original source.
04 / IDENTITY

Sometimes a belief becomes part of the group.

Beliefs do not exist only as isolated pieces of information. They can become connected to communities, political identities, social groups, and people's understanding of who they are.

A 2022 review examining social identity and conspiracy belief argues that conspiracy theories can interact with needs involving belonging, positive views of one's in-group, and security in group status. [5]

Once a claim becomes tied to identity, disagreement may therefore be interpreted as more than disagreement about evidence. It can be experienced as criticism of the group itself.

05 / DISTRUST

Distrust does not appear from nowhere.

Governments, corporations, intelligence agencies, researchers, media organizations, and other institutions have sometimes behaved dishonestly or unethically.

That history matters because people evaluate new claims through their previous understanding of institutional credibility.

But there is an essential logical distinction: evidence that an institution lied about one event does not prove that it is lying about another event.

Historical misconduct is evidence for the historical misconduct. A separate allegation requires separate evidence.

06 / DOCUMENTED CONSPIRACIES

Some secret institutional actions were real.

Responsible investigation cannot begin with the assumption that secret coordinated wrongdoing is impossible. There are documented historical cases in which institutions concealed, misrepresented, or secretly conducted activities.

PRIMARY SOURCE • FBI

COINTELPRO

The FBI's own historical account describes COINTELPRO and acknowledges that agents infiltrated groups, sowed discord, and attempted to discredit organizations, including cases where there was little or no evidence of unlawful activity. [6]

PRIMARY SOURCE • CDC

Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee

The CDC states that informed consent was not collected from participants and that treatment was not offered even after penicillin became the treatment of choice and was widely available. [7]

These examples establish an important principle: real institutional conspiracies and misconduct can occur. They do not establish that every allegation of conspiracy is credible.

07 / CORRECTIONS

Does correcting misinformation make people believe it more?

A popular claim says that correcting false information normally causes people to double down — the so-called backfire effect.

Current research does not support treating that as the normal response. A 2024 review concluded that factual corrections generally improve belief accuracy and that genuine backfire effects appear to be exceedingly rare. [8]

Another research review recommends detailed corrections, alternative explanations, reminders of the original misinformation, and repetition of the correction as useful strategies for improving correction effectiveness. [9]

Evidence Check Correcting misinformation is not hopeless. The quality, clarity, detail, source, and persistence of a correction matter.
08 / THE BLIND TRUTH STANDARD

Skepticism has to work in both directions.

Automatically believing an official explanation is not investigation.

Automatically rejecting an official explanation is not investigation either.

A serious investigation asks the same questions regardless of who makes the claim:

THE EVIDENCE TEST

  1. What exactly is being claimed?
  2. What is the original source of the claim?
  3. Is there primary evidence?
  4. Can the evidence be independently verified?
  5. Are multiple sources genuinely independent?
  6. What evidence contradicts the claim?
  7. What alternative explanations fit the evidence?
  8. What would prove the claim wrong?
  9. Are facts being separated from inference?
  10. How confident should we actually be?
09 / FINAL FINDING

Why do conspiracy theories refuse to die?

There is no single explanation.

Research indicates that conspiracy beliefs can interact with people's desire to understand uncertain events, feel secure, maintain control, and protect social identities. [1] [5]

Repetition can also increase perceived truth, meaning a claim may become more familiar — and therefore more believable — without becoming better supported by evidence. [2] [3] [4]

Historical cases such as COINTELPRO and the Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee demonstrate why institutional skepticism cannot simply be dismissed. [6] [7]

But skepticism is a method — not a predetermined conclusion. The existence of genuine historical wrongdoing cannot substitute for evidence supporting a different allegation.

Case File 001 Conclusion Question everything — including the conspiracy theory. Follow evidence wherever it leads, and change the conclusion when better evidence requires it.
